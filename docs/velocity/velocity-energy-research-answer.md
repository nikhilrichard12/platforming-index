## Velocity answer (completed overnight)

Bottom line

The folder supports a narrower pitch claim: across a mixed-sector survey, parts-sharing activity was more common than evidence of a governed, reusable product architecture, and energy-related case studies show that modular programs can remove substantial complexity. It does not establish how often energy-and-power manufacturers with duplicate parts have failed to achieve platforming at scale. The folder has no energy-only survey cross-tab and no dataset measuring both duplicate-part prevalence and platform maturity at the same firms. NOT IN SOURCES. Source: the-current-state-of-platforming-and-the-hidden-gap.md.

(b) Platform/modular architecture adoption

Kearney PERLab reports that only about half of its 100 mixed-sector respondents said they platform at scale. Even among those self-reporting scale, only a minority described a recent initiative with a common core and plug-in modules; most described parts sharing or activity outside product architecture. The article does not supply an exact percentage for that minority in its text. This is evidence of a definition and implementation gap, not an energy-sector adoption rate. Source: the-current-state-of-platforming-and-the-hidden-gap.md.

The same mixed-sector survey reports 43% naming engineering effort as the leading challenge; half identifying system architecture and platform planning as the step most needing improvement; and 52% of respondents who said they platform at scale lacking complexity targets in performance goals. These figures describe the survey respondents, not energy-and-power manufacturers separately. Source: the-current-state-of-platforming-and-the-hidden-gap.md.

For a concrete energy-related example of adoption, Wärtsilä’s engine-family case describes a modular platform after an earlier component-standardization approach could not deliver both variety and commonality. An anonymized utility subsidiary’s custom microgrid and data-center power-generation solutions were also redesigned around a shared chassis and modular options; that account gives no adoption rate or quantitative outcome. Sources: R2-Impact-by-function-R3-Existing-alternatives-folder-snapshot.md; product-architecture-is-a-boardroom-decision-not-just-an-engineering-choice.md.

Adoption percentage for power-generation equipment, grid/electrical equipment, or renewable-hardware manufacturers: NOT IN SOURCES. A folder snapshot mentions Siemens Gamesa wind-turbine platforms as an example, but reports no measured company outcome or sector adoption estimate. Source: R2-Impact-by-function-R3-Existing-alternatives-folder-snapshot.md.

Does this test the proposed claim? Partly, but not conclusively. The survey tests whether claimed platforming at scale corresponds to a common-core-and-modules description; it does not measure duplicate-part counts for those same companies. Wärtsilä shows a substantial opportunity to reduce component variety through modular architecture, but it is one successful case, not a count of manufacturers still lacking platforms. The microgrid account describes pre-program complexity but offers no quantified before-and-after figures. Sources: the-current-state-of-platforming-and-the-hidden-gap.md; R2-Impact-by-function-R3-Existing-alternatives-folder-snapshot.md; product-architecture-is-a-boardroom-decision-not-just-an-engineering-choice.md.

An energy-sector estimate of how many firms have both high part redundancy and no platform at scale is NOT IN SOURCES.” Source: the-current-state-of-platforming-and-the-hidden-gap.md; R2-Impact-by-function-R3-Existing-alternatives-folder-snapshot.md.
